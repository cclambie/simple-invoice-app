Across the world, public communication has come to depend on a handful of privately owned,
foreign-controlled platforms. Governments, journalists and citizens increasingly work inside systems
that put engagement, advertising and algorithmic amplification ahead of accuracy, accountability and
the public interest.

That creates structural risks: to democratic discourse, to national sovereignty, and to the integrity
of journalism itself.

The Fediverse, an ecosystem of interoperable platforms built on open standards like ActivityPub, is a
credible alternative that exists today. It isn't a new platform. It's a new model: institutions run
their own services while staying connected to a global network. For federal government and the media,
that's a strategic opportunity.

## 1. Restoring information sovereignty

Government communication shouldn't depend on outside platforms with opaque governance, shifting
policies and conflicting incentives. The Fediverse gives us:

- domestic control over communication infrastructure
- data kept within Australian jurisdiction
- independence from foreign-owned platforms and their APIs
- long-term stability through open standards
- no tracking, and no data sent to US-controlled servers

Federal agencies can run official servers for departments, public broadcasters and national
institutions, so communication carries on whatever a commercial platform decides. For journalism, it
reduces reliance on distribution systems that can suppress, amplify or monetise content at will.

## 2. Rebalancing power between platforms, media and the public

The platform model has weakened the relationship between journalists and their audiences. Algorithms
control distribution, intermediaries take the revenue, and misinformation and context collapse wear
away trust.

The Fediverse changes that:

- direct publisher-to-audience relationships
- chronological or community-controlled feeds
- no compulsory advertising or engagement-optimisation layer
- identity and audience you can take with you from server to server

Journalists and media organisations get back control over how their work is distributed and framed,
without losing reach.

## 3. Strengthening democratic communication

Public communication should be treated as critical infrastructure. Federated systems let governments:

- keep verified, authoritative channels outside commercial platforms
- deliver resilient public messaging during a crisis
- support plural, community-based discussion rather than one central narrative
- reduce exposure to coordinated manipulation and the disinformation that platform incentives reward

Because the Fediverse is distributed, it has no single point of failure, technical or political.

## 4. A sustainable future for journalism

Journalism is in a structural crisis, driven partly by dependence on platforms. The Fediverse makes new
models possible:

- subscriptions and memberships without a platform tax
- community-owned or publicly supported media servers
- interoperable publishing across institutions
- collaboration between public broadcasters, independent media and civic organisations

It's a shared layer of infrastructure where journalism doesn't have to bend to platform economics.

## 5. National resilience and security

Relying on a few global platforms brings systemic vulnerabilities: policy changes outside our control,
outages and restrictions, exposure to foreign influence operations, and moderation bottlenecks.

A federated approach spreads the risk across many independently run servers, with local governance,
shared standards and no central dependency. That lines up directly with national strategies for
critical infrastructure resilience and cyber security.

## 6. A practical path forward

None of this means walking away from existing platforms overnight. A phased approach is feasible and
low-risk:

1. Set up servers run by federal agencies or public broadcasters (Mastodon for posts, PeerTube for video).
2. Encourage media organisations to run their own.
3. Create inter-agency and public-media relays so people can find them.
4. Develop a verification framework for official accounts.
5. Support open standards across government communications.

It can start as a parallel system and grow into core infrastructure.

## 7. The strategic opportunity

The Fediverse brings together proven technology (ActivityPub is already widely deployed), growing
global adoption, rising dissatisfaction with platform concentration, and a clear fit with public policy
goals. Early movers, particularly at the federal level, get to shape the standards, norms and
governance, and can make sure they put democratic values first.

## The vision

A national communication ecosystem where:

- **Government speaks to citizens on infrastructure it helps govern:** it sets the moderation rules,
  shapes the culture of the community, and can block bad actors, bot networks and hostile states.
- **Journalists publish without algorithmic distortion:** less clickbait, less misinformation chasing
  engagement, and better journalism.
- **Public discussion is distributed, transparent and accountable.**
- **Institutions connect without giving up control,** because the Fediverse is federated.

This isn't a speculative future; it's already emerging. The EU is moving away from US-owned social
media for data sovereignty, and Australia should do the same. The question is whether our federal
institutions and media choose to take part, and to lead.
